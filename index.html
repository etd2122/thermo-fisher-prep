30-second version (for the recruiter)

"I wanted to settle who the greatest 100-meter sprinter is, but you can't just compare times because the sport changed so much over 50 years. So I measured dominance instead: how many standard deviations each runner beat the rest of the final by. That puts every era on the same scale. Usain Bolt came out on top, more than five standard deviations ahead of his fields. I presented it to faculty in Athens, and it's published on my GitHub."

The metric, in plain English

dominance = (runner's time − average time of the other finalists) / SD of the other finalists
  • Why not raw times? Track surfaces, shoes and timing technology improved, so a 10.0 in 1972 and a 10.0 in 2016 don't mean the same thing.
  • Why standard deviations? It measures how far ahead you were of the people you actually raced, in units that work for any year. Same idea as a z-score.
  • Why "the other finalists"? Each runner is compared against everyone else in the race, leaving themselves out, so a dominant winner doesn't drag the average toward their own time.

Explaining the code (if they ask "how did you build it?")

  1. Data. "I started from a public World Athletics database on GitHub, about 25,000 men's 100m results, and loaded it into R."
  2. Cleaning. "Results didn't come labeled by race, so I parsed a position code to work out round, heat and place, and built a race ID from venue, date and round. Rows I couldn't assign to a race I dropped and documented."
  3. Fixing gaps. "The database only lists times of 10.30 or faster, so older finals were missing their slowest runners. That would have made old fields look stronger than they were, so I hand-entered the missing finalists from official results, with a source for every row."
  4. Scoring. "I scored every finalist against the others, then ranked runners two ways: best single race and career average."
  5. Judgment calls. "I logged every assumption, like keeping doping-annulled races but flagging them, and excluding injured runners from field statistics, then tested how the rankings changed."
  6. Output. "Charts in ggplot2, some interactive, in a Quarto report on GitHub Pages."

Why you're interested · 30 seconds

"Prediction markets fascinate me because they turn opinions into prices. On Kalshi, a contract pays a dollar if something happens, so a price of 60 cents is basically the market saying there's a 60% chance. Sportsbooks set odds on the same games, so I'm comparing the two to see which forecasts NFL outcomes better and whether either one systematically misprices favorites and longshots. I'm also taking a Prediction Markets course this spring."

Key ideas to explain simply

Price = probability
A Kalshi contract trades between $0 and $1 and pays $1 if the event happens. The price is the crowd's implied probability. Sportsbook odds can be converted to an implied probability too.
The sportsbook's cut ("vig")
Sportsbook probabilities for both sides add up to more than 100%; the extra is the book's margin. To compare fairly with Kalshi, you remove that margin first. [Say only if this is a step in your project.]
Favorite-longshot bias
A well-documented pattern where bettors overpay for longshots, so longshots win less often than their prices imply and favorites win slightly more. You're testing whether sportsbooks, Kalshi, or both show it.
Your regression
"I'm building a price-gap test in R: the outcome is the difference between the two markets' implied probabilities, and the inputs are game factors, so I can see what drives the disagreement." [Name the factors you're actually testing.]

Being honest that it's ongoing

It's fine that you don't have results yet. Say: "It's ongoing; right now I'm [building the dataset / running the first regressions]. What I'm most curious about is [your hypothesis]." Never guess at findings you don't have.

Connecting it to Thermo Fisher

"Both markets are forecasts, and I'm measuring which forecast is better and explaining why they disagree. That's really what FP&A does: build forecasts, compare them with what actually happened, and explain the variance."
Framing tip: call it "prediction markets" and "forecasting," and talk about the data, not betting. It keeps the focus on the analysis.

06

FP&A brush-ups

Unlikely to be tested Monday, but likely in later rounds. Explain each in two sentences.

What does FP&A do?
Builds the budget, updates forecasts during the year, explains variances between actual results and plan, and supports investment decisions. Accounting records what happened; FP&A looks forward.
How the three statements connect
Income statement → net income Net income → top of cash flow statement → change in cash Ending cash + retained earnings → balance sheet (Assets = Liabilities + Equity)
Variance analysis
Budget $10.0M Actual $10.6M Variance +$0.6M (6% favorable) Volume: +400 units × $1,000 = $0.4M Price: higher price on existing units = $0.2M
Revenue above budget is favorable; costs above budget are unfavorable. The value is in explaining why.
Organic growth, with their numbers
Q3 2026 reported growth 9% − acquisitions/divestitures 3% − currency 0% = organic growth 6%
GAAP vs adjusted margin
Q3 2026 GAAP operating margin 18.6% vs adjusted 24.5%. The gap is mostly amortization of acquired intangibles and restructuring, typical for an acquisitive company.
Earn-outs (the Clario structure)
An earn-out is extra purchase price paid only if the acquired business hits targets. Clario's deal includes up to $400M tied to 2026–2027 performance. Finance tracks whether targets are likely to be met and adjusts the "contingent consideration" liability; Thermo Fisher's Q3 results mention net credits from such changes.
Free cash flow, EBITDA, working capital
FCF = operating cash flow − capex (2026 guidance: $7.0–7.5B). EBITDA = earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization. Working capital = receivables + inventory − payables; rising receivables tie up cash.
Budget vs forecast vs actuals
Budget is set before the year; forecasts update during it; actuals are what happened. Thermo Fisher's raised guidance is a public forecast update.

How to memorize your answers

Memorize the skeleton, not the script.

  1. Cut each story to four keywords, one per STAR step, e.g. "slump · rebuilt mechanics · film with coach · ERA dropped."
  2. Memorize only two lines word-for-word: the opening sentence and the result.
  3. Practice out loud. Silent rehearsal takes half the time and hides rambling.
  4. Active recall: see only the question, answer aloud, then check notes. The drill shuffles so order can't help you.
  5. Space it: short sessions Saturday, Sunday and Monday morning beat one cram.
  6. Record once per story and cut filler and anything over two minutes.
  7. Say it differently each time so you can bend a story to an unexpected question.
  8. Map five stories to every question in the bank.
On the call: a one-page keyword sheet beside your laptop is fine. Glance, don't read.
